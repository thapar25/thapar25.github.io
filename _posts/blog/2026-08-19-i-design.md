## Something Always Nagged

I spent most of my career on the backend. Agents, orchestration, inference pipelines, the stuff that doesn't show up on a screen.

But design kept pulling at me anyway.

Not in a "I secretly wanted to be a designer" way. More like a low hum in the background, every time I shipped something functional but ugly, some part of me flinched.

I noticed it most in conversations. I'd get more out of an hour with a UI/UX person than a week with a frontend team. Looking back, that's usually a sign you're in the wrong seat, or at least sitting one seat too far from the thing you actually care about.

## The College Project That Started It

Third year, an elective called Introduction to iOS Development with Swift 5. The presentation was for extra credit, which is the only reason it exists at all.

We built an NFC-enabled shopping and restaurant experience, tap a tag, skip the app install entirely. Minimal, fluid.

The presentation itself was Apple-themed, and that was the one I was genuinely proud of. Proud like I'd made something.

![Presentation Cover](/assets/images/internship-presentation.png)

That's when it clicked. I didn't just like using well-designed things. I liked making them.

## Took Me 26 Years

Here's the part that stings a little.

I've had an eye for design since that extra credit project. And it still took me twenty six years, my entire life so far, to actually ship a real website.

Exhibit one: a half-built site from my first year of college. Pure HTML, CSS, and JS. 2019. No framework, no design system, just vibes and syntax errors.

![Portfolio Website from College](/assets/images/portfolio-college.png)

Having an eye for something and having the tools to act on it are two very different problems.

## Paper Design, Then Claude Code

Fast forward. [xlr8labs](https://xlr8labs.in) needed a website, and this time I wasn't going to repeat 2019.

![Paper Design Screenshot](/assets/images/paper-design-screenshot.png)

I designed it in [Paper Design](https://paper.design/), which hooks into Claude Desktop through MCP, then built it out with Claude Code.

The harder part wasn't the tools. It was the vocabulary. I had to learn words like marquee and shaders just to describe what I wanted. ***You can't direct what you can't name.***

I'm hoping that vocabulary sticks around and makes me better at directing AI in general, not just for this one site. We'll see.

Tools like this lower the floor. You don't need years of design training to get something that looks intentional anymore.

But lowering the floor isn't the same as removing the ceiling.

## You Can Still Tell

Here's the part people don't want to hear.

You can still tell when a site is pure AI. Something about it reads as generic, even when every individual element is technically correct. The spacing is fine. The colors are fine. Nothing is wrong. Nothing is alive either.

Humans add the imperfections that make something feel considered. A weird margin that's actually intentional. A color choice that shouldn't work but does. Judgment calls that don't come from a training distribution.

The tools got good. The taste gap didn't close.

## Two Internets

The internet we built was for humans, and humans run on attention. Visuals, engagement loops, ad revenue. Every design decision of the last twenty years optimized for one thing: keep the eyes on the screen.

That internet is being outpaced.

Cloudflare's own CEO, admitting bots passed human traffic a year and a half before he predicted it would.

Agents don't have eyes to catch. They don't scroll, they don't get bored, they don't respond to a well-placed color contrast. An agent measures a page by how much value it can extract per token, how organized the content is, how fast it can find the answer and leave.

And now they're paying for that access.

Stablecoins, fractions of a cent, settled the moment the agent asks. A new economy denominated in amounts too small for a person to bother with, built entirely for machines that don't get tired of transacting.

Attention and value are not the same currency, and now literally not the same currency. A site optimized for one will actively fail at the other.

We're about to have two internets running on the same infrastructure. One built to hold human eyes. One built to be legible, and payable, to machines that don't have any.

The websites that survive both won't be the prettiest ones. They'll be the ones that figured out how to serve two audiences that want opposite things.
